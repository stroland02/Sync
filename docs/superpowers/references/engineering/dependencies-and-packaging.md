## 1. What this dimension covers, and why it matters here

Three questions that are usually treated as one and are not:

1. **Resolution** — how a version range becomes an exact tree, and whether that tree is recorded
   and enforced. A committed lockfile that CI does not enforce records nothing.
2. **Distribution** — how the thing reaches a user, and what a user can verify about what arrived.
3. **The extension seam** — if a third party writes a plugin, what do they install, and what do
   they inherit by installing it.

The third is the one Sync's business model rests on. Sync is open core. The moat is the substrate:
the API Dependency Graph plus the four protocols in `sync.core` that a vendor adapter is written
against. If writing a Twilio adapter means installing Postgres, LangGraph, tree-sitter and the
Claude Agent SDK, nobody writes one, and the open-core thesis is a licence file rather than a
strategy. Sync already treats this as an invariant rather than an aspiration — `sync.core` imports
nothing from a sibling, and a test enforces it. The packaging question is whether the *distribution*
keeps the promise the *import graph* makes, which is a strictly harder property: the import graph
is checked against source, and the distribution is checked against a wheel.

The second question matters for a different reason. Sync opens pull requests against customer
repositories, executes their toolchain, and installs their dependencies. A tool with that blast
radius that ships unsigned, unpinned, and unattested is asking a security-conscious buyer to take
it on faith. That is a sales problem before it is a security problem.

## 2. The design space across the nine

### Group A — vendor everything, trust nothing, verify continuously

**`codebase-memory-mcp`** is the most disciplined supply chain in the set and is not close.

It has **zero external build-time dependencies**. The C11 server compiles against six vendored C
libraries (`vendored/{mimalloc,nomic,sqlite3,tre,xxhash,yyjson}` — VERIFIED by `ls`) and 159
pre-generated tree-sitter grammars under `internal/cbm/vendored/grammars/`. `THIRD_PARTY.md` is a
per-component licence and provenance record; `internal/cbm/vendored/grammars/MANIFEST.md` records
for every grammar the upstream repository, the pinned commit, and a `verdict` field cross-verified
against two independent registries — nvim-treesitter's `parsers.lua` and Helix's `languages.toml`
(VERIFIED, MANIFEST.md summary block). The manifest is generated by a documented three-script
pipeline and states its capture date.

Three controls make that record load-bearing rather than decorative:

- `scripts/vendored-checksums.txt` (75 lines, VERIFIED) holds a SHA-256 per vendored file, and
  `scripts/security-vendored.sh` verifies them in CI. The script refuses to run without a hashing
  tool rather than passing vacuously — lines 32-42 carry the comment "Missing hashing is a hard
  failure: an integrity check that verified nothing must never pass or authorize a manifest update."
  It also rejects a symlinked manifest or vendored root before it starts (lines 22-29).
- `scripts/license-policy.json` is an SPDX allow-list with an explicit prohibition on adding
  copyleft without a maintainer decision, plus a justified `ignored_paths` list where each entry
  states why it is a false positive.
- **`scripts/license-gate.sh --selftest` plants a violation, runs the real gate, and fails if the
  gate did not catch it** (lines 20-37, VERIFIED). CI runs the self-test before the gate. This is
  Sync's own rule — "a test that cannot fail is worse than no test" — applied to a CI gate rather
  than to a pytest, and it runs on every release rather than once, by hand, when the gate was
  written.

Local patches to vendored sources are marked inline (`// CBM patch:`), explained in `THIRD_PARTY.md`
with the crash they prevent, and carry a bolded **"On re-vendor: re-apply this bound"** instruction.
Release (`.github/workflows/release.yml`, VERIFIED) produces `sha256sum` over the archives, an
`actions/attest-build-provenance` attestation, an `actions/attest-sbom` SBOM, and `cosign sign-blob`
signatures. 12 of the 18 `uses:` in that workflow are pinned to a 40-character commit SHA with the
tag in a trailing comment.

Distribution is eight channels from one source: `pkg/{aur,chocolatey,glama,go,homebrew,npm,pypi,scoop}`
plus `server.json` for the MCP registry — and `pkg/npm/test/package-publication.test.js` and
`pkg/npm/test/install-failure.test.js` mean the packaging itself has tests.

The cost is real and worth naming: 2012 files, most of them third-party C. The `dependabot.yml`
covers `github-actions` only, because there is no package ecosystem to cover.

### Group B — thin installer over a signed native artifact

**`open-code-review`** and **`codegraph`** solve the same problem — ship a compiled binary through
npm — and reach almost the same shape independently, which is a signal the shape is right.

`open-code-review/package.json` declares six per-platform `optionalDependencies` pinned exactly at
`0.0.0` (VERIFIED, lines 30-37), with the real version injected at publish
(`.github/workflows/release.yml:209`, `jq --arg v "$VERSION" '.version = $v'`). Its `postinstall`
runs `scripts/install.js`, which is the most careful installer I read:

- refuses any non-HTTPS URL outright (line 91-93) and caps redirects at 10 (line 94-96);
- downloads a `sha256sum.txt` from the release, recomputes the binary's digest, and **deletes the
  binary and throws on mismatch** (lines 197-238);
- **fails closed when no matching checksum entry exists** (lines 233-238) — the case a naive
  implementation treats as "nothing to check, carry on".

The release pipeline generates `sha256sum.txt` (line 145) and an `actions/attest-build-provenance`
attestation over it (line 156).

`codegraph` ships the same esbuild-style thin shim (`scripts/npm-shim.js`, `scripts/pack-npm.sh`,
both VERIFIED) but goes further on publish authenticity and on verification:

- **OIDC trusted publishing with no `NPM_TOKEN` at all**, plus explicit `npm publish --provenance`
  — the header comment at `release.yml:18-29` explains the explicit flag is kept so the run "fails
  loudly if OIDC is ever unavailable";
- a **post-publish verification loop** (`release.yml:273-287`) that polls the registry for every
  package with retries, because "npm publish can print success without persisting". That is
  `verification-before-completion` applied to a release, and I have not seen it anywhere else.

`codegraph` also vendors a Node runtime into the bundle (`BUNDLING.md`, VERIFIED) specifically to
delete `better-sqlite3` and with it every native addon, which is why "any target builds on any OS"
and the whole platform matrix runs on one Linux runner.

Where both fall short: `open-code-review` publishes npm packages **without** `--provenance`
(`release.yml:217` and `:240`, VERIFIED) even though it attests the GitHub-release binaries — so
the artefact a user actually installs from is the one carrying no attestation. And
`open-code-review` runs three package managers in one repository: Go modules at the root, npm for
the wrapper, and `yarn.lock` inside `extensions/vscode/` (VERIFIED via `git ls-files`), with no
lockfile at all for the npm wrapper.

### Group C — a workspace with a core package, unpublished

**`Understand-Anything`** is the closest structural analogue to Sync in the whole set, and reading
it is mostly a lesson in what goes wrong.

`pnpm-workspace.yaml` (VERIFIED) declares `understand-anything-plugin/packages/*`, and
`understand-anything-plugin/packages/core/package.json` is a real SDK-shaped package: 18 runtime
dependencies, and a six-entry `exports` map giving consumers `./search`, `./types`, `./schema`,
`./languages`, `./figma` as separate subpath entry points. `@understand-anything/skill` depends on
it as `"@understand-anything/core": "workspace:*"`.

Two supply-chain controls here are genuinely good and cheap:

- **`packageManager` carries a SHA-512 integrity hash** —
  `"pnpm@10.6.2+sha512.47870716…"` (package.json:28, VERIFIED). Corepack verifies the package
  manager binary itself before it resolves a single dependency.
- **`onlyBuiltDependencies` / `allowBuilds` is an explicit allow-list of packages permitted to run
  install scripts** (package.json:46-62 and pnpm-workspace.yaml, VERIFIED). Under pnpm, every other
  dependency's `postinstall` is blocked by default. Fourteen names are on the list, all of them
  tree-sitter grammars or `esbuild`/`sharp` — packages that genuinely need to compile. This is the
  single most effective npm supply-chain control available and it costs one array.

And then the failure: **nothing is published**. `.github/workflows/ci.yml` builds and tests
(VERIFIED); there is no publish workflow and no `npm publish` anywhere in the repository (VERIFIED
by grep across `*.yml`, `*.json`, `*.sh`, `*.mjs` — zero hits). The root package is `private: true`.
Distribution is `install.sh` / `install.ps1` copying a plugin directory. So the core/consumer split
is a build-time convenience, not a distribution boundary — and it shows in the versions:
`@understand-anything/core` sits at **0.1.0** while `@understand-anything/skill` is at **2.9.4**
(VERIFIED). The core version has never moved because nothing ever consumed it by version.

**INFERENCE:** this is what `workspace:*` costs once the two halves stop shipping together. A
floating intra-workspace pin cannot express "these two release in lock-step" because it never
expresses a version at all, so the version stops being maintained, and by the time you want to
publish the core separately there is no release history to publish against.

### Group D — a Python package with ranges and a lockfile nobody uses

**`code-review-graph`** publishes to PyPI and has the most thoughtful *range* discipline in the set.
`pyproject.toml:27-39` bounds every dependency on both sides — `mcp>=1.0.0,<3`,
`tree-sitter>=0.23.0,<1`, `networkx>=3.2,<4` — and the one non-obvious bound carries its reason
inline (lines 29-32): fastmcp `>=3.2.4` for three named CVE fixes, `<4` "prevents the next major
release from breaking the server the way fastmcp 3.0 did. See: #488". That is a comment stating a
constraint the code cannot show, which is exactly Sync's comment rule.

Optional dependencies are cleanly factored into seven extras with an `all` aggregate
(lines 52-79), so the embeddings stack and the Ollama wiki path are opt-in.

But: **`uv.lock` is committed (VERIFIED via `git ls-files`) and never used.** Every CI job installs
with `pip install -e ".[dev]"` (`ci.yml:22, 35, 48, 86, 101`, VERIFIED); the string `uv ` does not
appear in any workflow. So the lockfile is a file in the tree that resolves nothing, and every CI
run silently re-resolves against whatever PyPI serves that morning. Publishing uses a long-lived
`secrets.PYPI_API_TOKEN` with `twine upload` (`publish.yml`, VERIFIED) — no OIDC, no provenance,
in a repository that already has `dependabot.yml`, CodeQL, and a bandit config. The one credential
that could be compromised into supply-chain compromise is the one that was not modernised.

**`claude-cookbooks`** is the opposite balance and the reason it is worth reading despite being a
notebook collection: fifteen direct dependencies with floating lower bounds only, but
`uv sync --frozen --all-extras` at **three** separate workflow sites (`lint-format.yml:40`,
`notebook-quality.yml:37`, `notebook-tests.yml:40`, all VERIFIED). `--frozen` makes the committed
`uv.lock` the contract: CI fails if the lock does not match `pyproject.toml`, rather than silently
updating it. It also carries a `uv.toml` (VERIFIED, three lines) that pins the index explicitly:

```toml
[[index]]
url = "https://pypi.org/simple"
default = true
```

Cheap, and it removes the ambient-index-configuration class of failure — a `UV_INDEX_URL` in the
environment, or an inherited `pip.conf`, silently redirecting resolution.

### Group E — no packaging at all

**`PageIndex`** has a `requirements.txt` and nothing else. Nine lines (VERIFIED), mostly exact
(`litellm==1.84.0`, `pymupdf==1.26.4`), one unbounded (`regex>=2024.0.0`), no lockfile, no
`pyproject.toml`, no distribution. The README's only install instruction is
`pip3 install --upgrade -r requirements.txt` (README.md:156). Exact top-level pins with no lockfile
buy less than they appear to: `litellm==1.84.0` alone pulls a large transitive tree that is
unpinned and unrecorded, so two installs a week apart are different environments.

What it does have, and Sync does not, is `.github/workflows/dependency-review.yml` (VERIFIED) —
`actions/dependency-review-action@v4` with `fail-on-severity: moderate` on every PR to `main`. That
is roughly ten lines of YAML for a vulnerability gate on dependency *changes*, which is the moment
that matters.

**INFERENCE:** the packaging thinness is a business fact, not an oversight. The README leads with a
hosted platform, an MCP endpoint and an API. The repository is a demo of a SaaS. Sync should read
that as a warning rather than a model, because Sync's open-core thesis requires the opposite —
adoption of the substrate is the strategy.

### Group F — plugin marketplaces with no package manager

**`superpowers`** and **`skills`** distribute markdown and shell through the Claude Code plugin
marketplace. `superpowers/package.json` declares **zero dependencies of any kind** (VERIFIED);
`skills` has two devDependencies, both changesets. There is nothing to lock and nothing to audit.
Everything interesting is in version coherence, and the two land on opposite sides of it.

`superpowers` ships **`.version-bump.json`** (VERIFIED), which declares the seven places a version
string lives — `package.json`, four per-host `plugin.json` files, `gemini-extension.json`, and the
nested `plugins.0.version` inside `.claude-plugin/marketplace.json` — and `scripts/bump-version.sh`
(VERIFIED) with three modes: bump all, `--check` for drift across the declared set, and `--audit`
which additionally greps the whole repository for stale version strings with a configured exclude
list. The audit mode is the important one: it catches the *eighth* place, the one nobody declared.

`skills` uses changesets and **has drifted**: `package.json:3` says `1.1.0` and
`.claude-plugin/plugin.json:3` says `1.2.0` (both VERIFIED). `.changeset/config.json` sets
`privatePackages: { version: true }`, so changesets bumps `package.json` — and the plugin manifest,
which is the version a Claude Code user actually sees on install, is not in anything changesets
manages. The automated half moved and the visible half did not.

**`open-code-review`** has the same hazard structurally — root `package.json` at `0.0.0` and
`.claude-plugin/marketplace.json` at `1.0.0` — but defuses it by treating the manifest version as a
build-time placeholder injected at publish (`release.yml:209`, `:228`) rather than as a value
maintained by hand.

## 3. What Sync should adopt

Each item names the proof and the landing site.

### 3.1 `uv sync --frozen` in CI — do this first

**VERIFIED:** `uv.lock` is committed at the worktree root (1872 lines). `.github/workflows/ci.yml`
installs with `uv sync --all-extras --dev` at lines **66** and **241** — no `--frozen`, no
`--locked`. `uv sync` will re-resolve and rewrite `uv.lock` when it disagrees with
`pyproject.toml`, and the rewrite lives only in the runner. So a PR can add or bump a dependency,
go green against a resolution that exists nowhere in the repository, and merge; the next developer's
`uv sync` resolves something else. The lockfile is currently advisory.

Proof it works: `claude-cookbooks/.github/workflows/lint-format.yml:40`,
`notebook-quality.yml:37`, `notebook-tests.yml:40` — `uv sync --frozen --all-extras`.

Lands at: `ci.yml:66` and `ci.yml:241`. Two words. This is the highest value-per-character change
available in this dimension.

### 3.2 A self-testing gate, not only a self-tested test

**VERIFIED:** `codebase-memory-mcp/scripts/license-gate.sh:20-37` — `--selftest` creates a
directory holding an unlicensed `.c` file, invokes the real gate, and exits 1 unless the gate
printed `BLOCKED:` for the planted file. CI runs it before the gate itself.

Sync's `.claude/rules/test-discipline.md` already says "prove it detects a real violation before
trusting it — break the thing deliberately, watch the test go red, restore it", and the file records
that `test_import_boundary.py` once exited 0 without parsing its own argument. That rule asks a
human to do the check once. `codebase-memory-mcp` does it on every run.

Sync has three gates worth this treatment, all of which pass by *not* finding something and are
therefore in the same failure class:

- `tests/test_import_boundary.py` — already the near-miss;
- `tests/test_core_distribution.py::test_the_core_distribution_installs_and_works_without_the_runtime`
  — asserts `present_runtime_modules == []`, which is the empty-list-means-pass shape;
- `scripts/lint_encoding.py`, which `.claude/rules/test-discipline.md` names as the only defence
  against the missing-`encoding="utf-8"` class, and against which no fixture can be written.

The encoding lint is the one to do. **INFERENCE:** its whole justification is that no test can
catch what it catches, so nothing catches the lint itself being broken. A self-test that writes a
temporary `.py` file containing a bare `open(path)`, runs the lint, and fails unless the lint
flagged it would close that. It is a handful of lines and it is the check that guards a defect this
repository has shipped four times by its own count.

### 3.3 Pin the index

**VERIFIED:** `claude-cookbooks/uv.toml` — three lines declaring `https://pypi.org/simple` as the
default index. Sync has no `uv.toml` and no `[[tool.uv.index]]` block (VERIFIED by reading
`pyproject.toml` in full).

Lands at: a new `uv.toml` at the worktree root, or an equivalent block in `pyproject.toml`.
Removes a whole class of ambient-configuration failure in an environment (a customer's CI, a
contributor's laptop, a future container) that Sync does not control.

### 3.4 Declare where the version lives, and audit for the places you forgot

**VERIFIED:** the literal `0.1.0` appears in at least four places that must move together:
`pyproject.toml:3` (`version`), `pyproject.toml:43` (`sync-core==0.1.0`), `src/pyproject.toml:9`
(`version`), and `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:33-35`, which hardcodes
`dist/sync_core-0.1.0-py3-none-any.whl`, `dist/sync-0.1.0-py3-none-any.whl` and
`sync_core-0.1.0.dist-info/METADATA` in its verification snippet.
`tests/test_core_distribution.py:248` hardcodes the same dist-info path — that one at least fails
loudly on a bump, which is the good kind of coupling. The release doc does not: it would keep
printing a plausible command that reads a file that no longer exists.

Proof: `superpowers/.version-bump.json` plus `scripts/bump-version.sh --audit` (both VERIFIED).
Counter-proof of what happens without it: `skills` at `1.1.0`/`1.2.0` (VERIFIED).

Lands at: a `.version-bump.json`-shaped declaration listing the four sites, plus a
`--audit`-equivalent grep. **INFERENCE:** given Sync's `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:54-57` explicitly
leaves "do the two versions move together" open as a release-policy decision, this tooling should
exist *before* that decision is made, because whichever way it goes the set of files that must agree
is the same set.

### 3.5 A dependency-change gate

**VERIFIED:** Sync's `.github/` holds `ISSUE_TEMPLATE`, `PULL_REQUEST_TEMPLATE.md`, and
`workflows/ci.yml`. There is no `dependabot.yml` and nothing in CI inspects a dependency for a known
vulnerability.

Cheapest proven option: `PageIndex/.github/workflows/dependency-review.yml` — `actions/dependency-review-action@v4`,
`fail-on-severity: moderate`, on `pull_request` to `main`. Twenty lines. It gates *changes* rather
than nagging about the whole tree, which for a solo founder is the right trade.

`codebase-memory-mcp`, `code-review-graph`, `open-code-review` and `PageIndex` all carry a
`dependabot.yml`; the two I read (`codebase-memory-mcp`, `PageIndex`) cover `github-actions` only,
which is itself worth noting — Sync's CI pins `astral-sh/setup-uv@v5`, a floating major tag, and
`actions/checkout` similarly. `codebase-memory-mcp` pins 12 of 18 `uses:` in its release workflow to
a 40-character SHA with the tag in a trailing comment (VERIFIED). Sync should do that for the
publish workflow when it writes one, and can leave `ci.yml` on tags.

### 3.6 When the publish workflow is written, start at the good end

**VERIFIED:** `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:1-5` — "Nothing here publishes. Publishing is public and
irreversible and is a decision a human makes." That is a defensible position and the checklist
beneath it is good. It means the first publish workflow is unwritten, which is the cheapest moment
to get it right.

Copy from `codegraph/.github/workflows/release.yml` (VERIFIED), in this order of value:

1. **OIDC trusted publishing, no long-lived token.** `release.yml:18-29` and `:249-271`. PyPI
   supports this for GitHub Actions. `code-review-graph/publish.yml` is the counter-example — a
   long-lived `PYPI_API_TOKEN` in a repository that is otherwise security-conscious.
2. **`--provenance` / attestation, explicitly, even where it is implied**, so an OIDC failure is
   loud rather than a silent downgrade (`release.yml:256-258` carries exactly this reasoning).
3. **Verify against the registry after publishing, with retries** (`release.yml:273-287`). Sync's
   own `verification-before-completion` discipline says a success claim needs evidence; a publish
   step's exit code is not evidence that the artifact is fetchable.

### 3.7 Two smaller things worth taking

- **Bound the upper end where a break is known.** `code-review-graph/pyproject.toml:29-32` is the
  model: two-sided bound, plus a comment naming the CVEs the lower bound fixes and the incident the
  upper bound prevents. Sync's fifteen runtime dependencies are all `>=` with no ceiling
  (`pyproject.toml:23-48`, VERIFIED). Adding blanket `<N+1` caps would be worse than useless — it
  makes every major bump a manifest edit and trains people to raise the cap without reading. But
  the two where a break has already been felt deserve the treatment, with the reason inline.
- **The install-script allow-list has a Python analogue worth thinking about.**
  `Understand-Anything`'s `onlyBuiltDependencies` (VERIFIED) blocks arbitrary `postinstall`
  execution by default. Sync installs *customer* dependencies during VERIFY, and `CLAUDE.md`
  records that installs already pass `--ignore-scripts`, which is the same control, correctly
  applied. Worth noting that Sync got the harder case right; the gap is on its own dev-time
  installs, where `uv` runs arbitrary build backends from source distributions and nothing
  currently constrains that.

## 4. Where Sync is already ahead, and where a reference would be a step backwards

### 4.1 The two-distribution split is better than all nine

**VERIFIED**, and this is the significant result of the audit. Sync builds two distributions from
one tree, and the arrangement is more rigorous than anything in the reference set:

- `pyproject.toml:67-71` — `[tool.uv.workspace] members = ["src"]` with
  `[tool.uv.sources] sync-core = { workspace = true }`, so development resolves core from the tree
  and a published `sync` resolves it from the index.
- `pyproject.toml:73-77` — `wheel-exclude = ["core/**"]`, with a comment stating the constraint the
  code cannot show: the two distributions must never own the same files, "uninstalling either would
  otherwise take core out from under the other."
- `src/pyproject.toml:56-63` — `module-root = ""`, `module-name = "sync.core"`, `namespace = true`.
  `sync` is a regular package in the repository and a namespace package in the core distribution,
  which is what lets an environment hold core alone without owning the `sync` name.
- `src/pyproject.toml:42-44` — **one** dependency, `pydantic>=2.13.4`.
- `pyproject.toml:43` — `sync-core==0.1.0`, exact, because the two release together.

`Understand-Anything` is the only reference attempting the same shape and it is unpublished, pinned
`workspace:*`, and its core version has been stranded at `0.1.0` while its consumer reached `2.9.4`
(all VERIFIED). Everything else either publishes one artifact (`code-review-graph`, `codegraph`,
`PageIndex`) or has no library seam at all.

What actually puts Sync ahead is not the split but the **enforcement**.
`tests/test_core_distribution.py::test_the_core_distribution_installs_and_works_without_the_runtime`
(VERIFIED, lines 142-190) builds the core wheel, creates an empty 3.12 venv, installs the wheel
alone, and runs a probe inside it that imports all five protocols, exercises `check_vendor_adapter`
in **both** directions — accepting a conforming adapter and refusing one with no `vendor_id`, with
the comment "a kit that certifies everything is indistinguishable from a kit that ran" — and then
asserts that eleven named runtime modules and nine sibling packages are absent, and that the `sync`
distribution is not installed. No reference in this set tests a packaging property by building the
artifact and using it in isolation. The closest is
`codebase-memory-mcp/pkg/npm/test/package-publication.test.js`, which I did not read line by line
but which by name tests the packaging rather than an installed artifact's behaviour.

Sync also solved a legal defect none of the nine addressed: `src/pyproject.toml:19-32` explains that
`License-Expression` alone ships no licence text, that PEP 639 forbids `..` in a `license-files`
glob so the text must be duplicated under `src/`, and that
`test_core_distribution.py:193-212` asserts the two copies are byte-identical so the duplication
cannot drift. That reasoning chain — a legal requirement, a tooling constraint that forces a
duplicate, and a test that makes the duplicate safe — is better than anything in the references.

**Do not regress toward `workspace:*` or its Python equivalent (a bare `sync-core` with no
specifier) for developer convenience.** `Understand-Anything` shows the cost: the version stops
being a fact anyone maintains.

### 4.2 Where a reference's approach would cost more than it returns

- **`codebase-memory-mcp`-scale vendoring is wrong for Sync.** It costs 2012 files and a
  re-vendoring protocol per component. Sync's dependencies are Python packages from one index, not
  C sources compiled into the binary, and Sync ships a service rather than a static binary. Take the
  three *controls* — checksummed vendored inputs where anything is vendored, an SPDX allow-list, a
  self-testing gate — and none of the vendoring.
- **`codegraph`'s vendored-Node bundle solves a problem Sync does not have.** It exists to delete
  native addons and Node-version dependence for a `curl | sh` install. Sync is a Python service with
  a Postgres dependency; there is no equivalent single-binary story, and constructing one would be
  a large project against no current demand.
- **`code-review-graph`'s seven optional-dependency extras would be premature.** Extras earn their
  cost when a heavy dependency is genuinely optional. Sync's fifteen runtime dependencies are
  currently all required by the pipeline. The one place the pattern may land later is the console:
  `starlette` and `uvicorn` are only needed by the read-only transport, and a headless deployment
  running the pipeline alone does not need either. That is a real future extra; it is not one today.
- **`PageIndex`'s exact-pins-without-a-lockfile is strictly worse than what Sync has.** Sync's
  `uv.lock` records the full transitive resolution. Do not migrate toward top-level `==` pins;
  enforce the lock instead (3.1).

### 4.3 One defect found in Sync's own manifest

**VERIFIED**, `pyproject.toml:39-43`:

```toml
    # The plugin SDK, built from `src/pyproject.toml` out of the same tree. Pinned exactly
    # because the two are released together and `sync` imports core's models directly, so a
    # runtime paired with a core it was not built against is a shape nobody tested.
    "starlette>=0.49.0",
    "sync-core==0.1.0",
```

The comment explaining the exact pin now sits above `starlette`, not above `sync-core`. The
dependency list is alphabetised, `starlette` sorts before `sync-core`, and whoever added the console
transport inserted it between the comment and its subject. A reader arrives at the one dependency in
the file whose specifier is load-bearing and finds the explanation attached to the wrong line — and
worse, finds `starlette` apparently described as "the plugin SDK". One-line fix: move the comment
below `starlette`. Worth doing because this comment is the only thing in the manifest that explains
why `==` rather than `>=`, and `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:54-57` treats that pin as an open
release-policy question that someone will come back to read.

## 5. Open questions only the owner can settle

1. **Is `sync-core` registered on PyPI yet?** **INFERENCE** — I have no network and did not check.
   `pyproject.toml:43` declares `sync-core==0.1.0` as a dependency of the published `sync`, and
   `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:61-62` says nothing has been uploaded even to TestPyPI. Until the
   name is claimed, anyone can register `sync-core` on PyPI, and a user who installs `sync` from the
   index resolves *their* package. Registering both names — even with a placeholder 0.0.0 — costs
   one upload and closes a hijack that would be indistinguishable from a legitimate install. This is
   the one item here with a deadline attached, because it stops being cheap the moment the first
   person tries `pip install sync`.

2. **Do the two versions move together, or does the pin become a range?**
   `docs/releasing-sync-core.md:54-57` leaves this open deliberately. It is a real fork.
   Lock-step (`==`) means an adapter author's `sync-core` upgrade is forced by every runtime
   release, which is friction on exactly the population the open-core thesis needs. A range means
   accepting untested pairings. **INFERENCE:** the middle path is a compatible-release specifier on
   core's *minor* line (`sync-core~=0.1`) once core's protocol surface stabilises, plus a
   conformance-kit version check — but nothing in this repository is stable enough for that yet, and
   `==` is the right answer until the fifth adapter exists.

3. **Does the console's dependency split matter enough to become an extra?** `starlette` and
   `uvicorn` serve the read-only transport only. If Sync is ever deployed headless — a CI-only
   pipeline with no console — those two are dead weight. Whether that deployment shape exists is a
   product question, not a packaging one.

4. **How much supply-chain ceremony does the buyer actually ask for?** Sync opens pull requests
   against customer repositories and executes their toolchain. **INFERENCE:** signed releases and
   an SBOM will eventually be a procurement checkbox rather than a nicety. `codebase-memory-mcp`
   shows the full apparatus (cosign, `attest-sbom`, SHA-pinned actions) and `codegraph` shows the
   80% version (OIDC + `--provenance`, no cosign). The full apparatus is not worth a solo founder's
   week today; the 80% version costs about an hour and is worth doing with the first publish
   workflow. Which one, and when, depends on who the first paying customer is.

5. **Is the encoding lint's self-test worth the precedent?** Adding a self-test to one gate invites
   adding one to all of them, and a self-test is itself untested code. **INFERENCE:** the encoding
   lint is the strongest case in the repository — `.claude/rules/test-discipline.md` states plainly
   that no test can cover what it covers, and the defect it guards has shipped repeatedly. If the
   precedent is unwelcome, do that one and write down why the others do not qualify.
